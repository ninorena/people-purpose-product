Over the past few months, I have had the pleasure of watching someone on a client team grow into the product owner role. And in a world jam packed with frameworks, tools, and approaches, I was happy to see the mental model of experimentation become a heavy influence in how that product owner addresses her work. Being the player/coach I am, I have gotten to walk this journey with her and it's been yet another reminder that, even in this AI-hype cycle we live in, slow is smooth, and smooth is fast.

Here's how we work:

- I ask her to write down what she believes about the product we are working on, and what she doubts.
- We turn her notes into questions, and vote on which one needs to be answered first.
- That question turns into an experiment that dictates how we gather data to answer it, and guards space on our calendars to glean insights that tell us what to do next.

Just a few experiments into working this way, the conversation changed. She started asking how we could run them faster, which is always a welcome ask from my side seeing as I'm usually the one asking to move faster!

Throughout my career I have aimed to give the teams I work on or with more agency over what they ask of a product or solution. I want them to need less from the opinions of others (including me) and let their own curiosities drive product development.

### The role and the job

I think often back to this old Melissa Perri article in which she makes an important distinction. [Product owner is a role. Product manager is the job.](https://medium.com/@melissaperri/product-manager-vs-product-owner-57ff829aa74d)

I find this distinction even more relevant today, what with the undeniable AI hype train traveling through all of our towns. Organizations with a theory of change, and who have a limited digital product footprint, are all of a sudden applying for and winning grants that ask them to experiment with AI. Leaders in those organizations are all of a sudden thrust into this product owner role without actually having the job of product manager. In an impact-focused organization, the product owner is often the leader who knows the community best, and that person usually makes the final product calls even though they have never been taught the job that sits behind the call.

If you know someone who has assumed the role of product owner without the job of product manager, you probably can relate to the sound of "I know the problems my community faces. I do not know how a product can solve them well, or how to prioritize what to build first." That gap has a cost because the team that builds the product and the people inside the organization expect different things. Some ask for too much. Some ask for too little. Some do not ask the questions that matter. But that certainly doesn't mean that there isn't merit to the ideas being shared.

I think it's clear that the job can be learned, and the product owner I described above is learning it the way most people do: one experiment at a time. The journey can be hard at times, like when we have to test with real users, but the satisfaction of learning something real only fuels the repetitions required to really practice the job.

### What didn't get cheaper

Another oldie but goodie, and one that feeds my fancy for first principles: in his book "Essentialism", Greg McKeown points out that the word "priority" did not have a plural until quite recently:

> "The word priority came into the English language in the 1400's. It was singular. It meant the very first or prior thing. It stayed singular for the next 500 years. Only in the 1900s did we pluralize the term and start talking about priorities."

I love running experiments because an experiment brings the word "priorities" back to the singular. One experiment answers one question, so you have to pick the question first. And running an experiment means someone has to say no A LOT, and own the one priority that is left. That is incredibly uncomfortable, and I find that wading through that discomfort is the crux of learning the job of a product manager.

When AI lets us output so much more at the click of a button, it can easily feel that the whole process of building working products got cheaper. However, most of what got cheaper is just the "brick-laying". A prototype, a new page, or a feature that used to take a sprint can now take an afternoon.

Learning what real users want/use/need did not get cheaper. You still need to find the right people, put the real product in their hands, and watch what they do with it. Then you need time on the calendar to make sense of what you saw. Users still have only 24 hours in a day, and so does the product owner.

So the success of a product now depends even more on the taste of the product owner, a taste that is developed through iterations of real experiments put in the hands of real users. Deciding what goes in and what stays out did not get cheaper.

### The first skill is "I don't know"

If you find yourself in this position, what can you learn first to become a more informed product owner?

**The question comes before the build.** Get comfortable asking the questions you think your product can answer. Then say "I don't know" when you actually do not know the answer. Sometimes you also do not know how a product can answer the question. Say that too.

The same goes for the field itself. Nobody knows where AI tools will push software in the next few years, and I don't either. So instead of trying to predict an uncertain future, I would rather rely on the discipline of making small bets, setting a pass/fail condition in advance, and leaving enough budget to still be around in a few years to find out if the bet paid off. Slow is smooth, and smooth is fast.

And it will be uncomfortable, because the work in front of you isn't going to match the volume of your vision every single day. Even if you prioritize the wrong thing, a timeboxed bet like the ones in that client's work lets you backtrack. A bet that teaches you something unexpected is better than no bet at all.
