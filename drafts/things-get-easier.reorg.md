It's been a while since I published a new post, and that's because my wife and I recently welcomed our son Noah into the world :)

Entering parenthood, I noticed something right away: whenever I shared what our days all of a sudden looked like, people would always offer: "Things get easier." Well…thank goodness! The diapers, the spit-up, the sporadic sleep, all of that eventually cools off (well, maybe not so much the sporadic sleep…). I'm certainly glad this parenting cliché holds up.

Let me be clear: if I could impress upon Noah that there are other ways to ask for things outside of crying, I would. But while we weather this phase, it's clear that everything I do is now buoyed by a profound sense of purpose, and a clarity I have never felt. And while these don't quite give me more hours of sleep, so much is easier now than it has ever been.

The effort is the same. What's gone is the wondering whether it's worth it.

## Products as a Vehicle for Purpose

I can't help but reflect on the work I do helping teams build products that bring their purpose to the world and how the same first principles hold true. When an organization has their purpose clearly articulated, and the discipline to ensure everything they do is in service to that purpose, the resulting clarity is transformational. Decisions about how to measure outcomes and impact get easier. Value proposition framing gets clearer. And most importantly, our ability to prioritize how we spend our time building gets so much easier.

> **[NOTE 2 — one word to eyeball]** "the resulting clarity is **transformational**" sits close to the applause-word line (per your own write-like-a-human list). You earn big claims with argument elsewhere, so this one adjective might be the outlier. Your call.

Early in my career, I participated in entrepreneurial communities of practice where I really got to appreciate that the biggest risk any product faces isn't that it's built poorly. It's that it was built to solve the wrong thing for the wrong person. Products fail for all kinds of technical reasons, sure, but the goal that always stuck with me was prioritizing the right thing to build. Fighting against our own biases to build a solution that is truly in service to its users requires a great deal of clarity, and the discipline to say no to the distractions that pull a roadmap in six different directions.

Here's where it shows up most for me. I've always thought of prioritization as an exercise in saying no, not saying yes, and that framing has served me well for years. But figuring out what to say no to is so much harder than figuring out what to say yes to. Saying yes is easy; the world is full of good things to say yes to. Saying no is where the real work lives.

That has gotten easier too since I met the little guy. I know where my time and energy belong now, with a clarity I didn't have just a few months ago. The nos come faster because the yes underneath them, him, is never in question.
